Together, Less Is More

I've played guitar for the bulk of my life. From what my parents tell me, I wanted one even as a toddler; thanks to a roundabout deal I made with my mother, I didn't actually learn to play until I was 12 years old. I was obsessed with the instrument. My sister played first and actually owned her own guitar -- a fact I thought was rather unfair -- before I got one on my own. After my father purchased her a book on playing, I would sneak it (and the guitar) to practice with when she wasn't home.

If memory serves me right, I ended up with a very entry-level acoustic at some point. I set out to play as much as I could, and while I wasn't immediately great (I'm still not where I'd like to be!), there was something pushing me to take it as far as I could. As a 12 year old, that meant a few things that -- in hindsight -- would make me chuckle today (or horrify a music teacher). For example, I favoured steel strings because I liked the bright crisp sound (which were painful compared to nylon strings). I knew nothing about string tension or proper action -- let alone whether they mattered on an acoustic or electric -- so I suspect my beginner setup was quite poor.

I do remember trading that acoustic away for a Squier Telecaster that I still have to this day. It was a Kijiji trade: I think I forked over my acoustic and $100 (my hard earned paper route money!) for it. I remember that my mother was not happy about the business transaction, but I was over the moon. I was one step closer to arriving at my dreams of playing what I wanted to play.

I learned fairly quickly that while I enjoyed lead guitarists -- Ben Kasica of Skillet, for one -- I found a sense of joy in rhythm guitarists. Chalk it up to teenage impatience at learning proper (guitar) music theory and fundamentals: while some of my mates pushed themselves to shred, I started trying to think more selflessly with the 6 strings. To be fair, some of my favourite guitarists do count as lead guitarists with incredible technical chops. As a teenager it was Ian D'sa of Billy Talent and Dave Baksh (Sum 41) -- Canadian powerhouses! As my music taste got heavier and went from pop punk to alternative to post-hardcore to metalcore, the ethos of "less might be more" started to really drive my approach to playing. I was enamoured with Brad Delson of Linkin Park -- I actually attribute my love of over-the-ear headphones to wanting to be just like him as a teen -- and his tasteful approach to playing. I couldn't understand how someone could alternate between the aggressiveness of Hybrid Theory and the wall of tone in Meteora while still having space for the melodies and chimes in Minutes to Midnight. To this day I'm not sure if it's a direct quote from him or a paraphrase on his Wikipedia page from back in the day, but learning that Delson disliked soloing and drawing attention to himself -- to use an awful pun -- struck a chord with me. And so that became my approach to playing: less is more.

In theory.

You see, having spent so much of my life with an electric guitar and without an amp to match it, there were some very important fundamental lessons I missed out on till I eventually did purchase an amp. Three of those lessons go hand in hand. If you're a guitarist, you will roll your eyes at how relatively recently I learned them. I won't say when exactly, but for someone who enjoys reading about music creation and watching documentaries/vlogs of his favourite artists in the studio, it's embarrassing how long it's taken to learn the lessons.

(I will switch tenses as if I was chatting with my younger self for a moment.)

Hello, past me!

First, the gain knob on the amp? Yes, that one! I see you've turned it up and you think it sounds incredible in the church basement! Can you do me a favour and turn it down by a third? Yes, I'm serious.

Secondly (and this follows from the first): you keep trying to imitate the sounds you hear on the radio or on Rdio or on YouTube. I would like to take a seat as I tell you something important: you will not be able to recreate those tones with one guitar. Even if you nail the tone you think you're hearing, you're not nailing it. The tone you hear is rarely one guitar (or 2-3 if it's tracked a certain way), but most likely multiple guitar takes and attempts placed together with various equalizers, pedals, and other effects in the signal chain. And man, if you think that emulating analog gear is challenging, wait until you realize that some of the artists you listen to (and will listen to!) create their tone digitally with software. Good luck copying that with one amp!

Lastly, speaking of equalizers: there's a bass knob on the amp. If you're playing alone, sure, use it. When you play with other musicians and especially when there's a drummer and/or bassist (whether at school or your classmate's basement or at church), turn it down. This will become very important as you get into heavier music and try to recreate the sounds your favourite bands use.

(Right. Back to the present.)

And now, in the present again.

As someone who beats himself up about things a little too much, I actually don't do much of that when I reflect on these three things. Sure, I'm a bit sheepish at how long it took me to learn them. Yes, it's possible that in the rapid learning I did in junior high/high school (where I'd race through my homework and then pester my older friends about their gear and being in a band and lurk music forums to soak up all the knowledge I could) I learned these things and promptly forgot, but it's okay. The 'head knowledge' I got from reading forums or articles or having chats with friends is one thing: the 'hand knowledge' I got in owning an amp a for the last few years (and/or accidentally stumbling into the world of high-fidelity audio) is another. And someday, Lord willing, I'll own a tube amp instead of a digital modeller, and everything I've only read about them (or seen in YouTube videos, or experienced for a few minutes at local music stores) will become my everyday experience! I can't be angry with myself for not knowing what I didn't know. But now, I know.

Where was I? Oh, right. Less might be more.

A Technical Detour (I'm so sorry)

Given that I'm not a signals engineer, I will not try to explain what gain is in great detail.

(As an aside: if you must ask, without me looking it up in a new tab as I write this draft, gain occurs in the power section of an amplifier: the power amp -- rather redundantly -- amplifies the guitar's signal above a certain level, so the guitar effectively "gains" volume. If enough of it is "gained", the signal starts to clip and we say that the signal is no longer a clean signal but a...distorted signal. You'll never guess what we call the effect when enough of that clipping occurs to the signal/waveform.)

I can provide a link to a few videos for the YouTube or TikTok audience that want to know the technical explanation of gain and what it does for a guitar's signal, but I will default to the explanation that I used (rather clumsily) for the bulk of my life: gain is what helps the electric guitar sound...well...like that. Pick your genre of guitar player: there's probably at least some gain in their sound, but my goodness - it's actually much less than I thought it was.

When playing on your own, more gain makes for a thicker fatter sound.(It also hides the mistakes one makes while playing, and there's an argument to be made that if you want to become a better guitarist you should use less gain so you hear the mistakes you make, but that's besides the point of this piece.) That thicker sound might sound to your ears, but take it to its next conclusion: imagine if there were two of you playing that same way with the gain cranked up. Three of you. Four of you. N + 1 of you. That signal would become a mess to deal with, and even the most equipped audio engineers would be vexed at your collective mess of sound.

(Alternatively, they might chop off some frequencies in the audio spectrum and call it fuzz or shoegaze, but nevertheless...).

If the music descriptions aren't helpful, think of a balanced soup. I'll use minestrone because it's a personal favourite. The joy of a great soup is the balance of everything. I want the beans, I want the spice, I want the vegetables. In a chili, the ratio of beans to beef to carrots to onions to tomatoes to seasoning matters just so. Yes, you can go overboard on one or the other. But together...less of each ingredient makes more of a delicious pot.

You get the picture. Too many cooks with too much gain is a muddy mess. There's exceptions. But it probably helps you to have a little less gain than you think you should be using -- especially if you play with 2+ guitarists, and very much so if you play with a bassist and drummer.

If you think back to your music days in elementary school, you'll recall the musical staff: there's a bass clef and a treble clef, and we say some voices sit in some parts of the piece while others span a different range. A band is the same thing, but now we're thinking in terms of frequencies too - perhaps we should add high school or university physics to the mix. Sound is frequency, and frequencies have spectrums. A bass guitar has a certain frequency it occupies: a guitar has a certain range it lives in. Human vocals occupy a specific range, and drums do too. You could stick everyone in the same spectrum and have a muddled mix, but there's a bit more value in having each instrument occupy its own frequency -- or at least, try its best to. When I play a 7 string guitar, I recognize that as I go lower in tuning, I'll start to encroach on the bass guitar's range. When the audio engineer hears the mix and sees all the parts laid out, they can decide to make some design decisions: should she filter out more of the guitar's low end to allow space for the bass's high end? Or should a mixing engineer trust his eyes and his ears and allow some of the guitar's heaviness to remain while scooping out some of the bass, but elect to keep enough of the drums to bring back the thump?

To riff on the old quote: it is a truth universally recognized that showboating guitarists take far too long to learn, but the heaviness in a lot of music we enjoy is actually delivered by the bass and the drums, not the guitar.

Next: the trickery of music production

We should know by now that music videos aren't always the real thing, but the more horrifying news for me was learning that what might sound like one or two guitars is probably multiple guitar tracks done on multiple takes. Music is a lie! It's all a lie! But it's not. But someone with taste -- the musicians, the engineers, the session artists -- they come together and their 'less' becomes more with the final song. It also explains why what I heard growing up -- the full piece of work over weeks or months that many hands had created -- never quite sounded like my individual attempts.

To really bury that point home in a really nihilistic way: unless I had the ability to have the exact same guitar(s)(for come on -- who thinks they're tracking with one guitar these days!) and the exact same signal chain and the exact same strings and the exact same picks and the exact same audio interfaces and the exact same audio engineers and the exact same studio and the exact same microphones to amp or reamp the sounds-

I could go on. The point stands. The tone, let alone the mix, wouldn't be the same, and I have learned to make my peace with how freeing that is.

From Music to Community

There's a temptation to do a lot. To be a lot. To output a lot. To create a lot. I feel it daily. I will fight it and probably will fight it my entire life. I don't think I'm doing enough. I've been told by parents and peers and friends and teachers and managers and coworkers and clients at various times or another that I need to do more. They have all been right at times, and this isn't entirely about that. To do things in life a healthy amount of push and "doing too much" is important.

But in the context of making things together - I think there's a lovely argument to be taken from the peace I found when I finally realized just how difficult it might be to make it all -- however you define 'it' to be -- on our own. Growing up, my father would always remind me "No man is an island". There is a hyper-individualism that runs through our world today and while it has its merits at times -- for there's a great joy in being able to be self-sufficient -- there's also a horror that comes with it. We forget to ask for help. We sneer at those who don't make it like we did (forgetting that we probably had some support too!). And when we do try to do it, we do too much and we can't sustain it.

It's quite difficult to keep up with everything that happens today. But it might be easier to keep up with one area that we hold dear so we can inform our loved ones about it -- and imagine if they did the same! We think to ourselves that we need to correct everything and anything right away and burn out at both ends trying to correct inaction instead of taking a more measured and sustained approach...or maybe that's my projection.

Whether they be your future self/ves or others, maybe there's value in leaving room for the other band members and allowing them to shine without dragging them for not knowing to turn the gain down or whatever they hadn't learned yet. The change that sticks and the sound that stays -- whether with you personally or in the communities you exist in -- might just be found in adopting the approach that less is more.
