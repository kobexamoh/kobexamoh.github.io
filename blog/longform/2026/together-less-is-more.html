Together, Less Is More

I've played guitar for the bulk of my life. From what my parents tell me, I wanted one even as a toddler; thanks to a roundabout deal I made with my mother, I didn't actually learn to play until I was 12 years old. I was obsessed with the instrument. My sister played first and actually owned her own guitar -- a fact I thought was rather unfair -- before I got one on my own. After my father purchased her a book on playing, I would sneak it (and the guitar) to practice with when she wasn't home.

If memory serves me right, I ended up with a very entry-level acousticat some point. I set out to play as much as I could, and while I wasn't immediately great (I'm still not where I'd like to be!), there was something pushing me to take it as far as I could. As a 12 year old, that meant a few things that -- in hindsight -- would make me chuckle today (or horrify a music teacher). For example, I favoured steel strings because I liked the bright crisp sound (which were painful compared to nylon strings). I knew nothing about string tension or proper action -- let alone whether they mattered on an acoustic or electric -- so I suspect my beginner setup was quite poor.

I do remember trading that acoustic away for a Squier Telecaster that I still have to this day. It was a Kijiji trade: I think I forked over my acoustic and $100 (my hard earned paper route money!) for it. I remember that my mother was not happy about the business transaction, but I was over the moon. I was one step closer to arriving at my dreams of playing what I wanted to play.

I learned fairly quickly that while I enjoyed lead guitarists -- Ben Kasica of Skillet, for one -- I found a sense of joy in rhythm guitarists. Chalk it up to teenage impatience at learning proper (guitar) music theory and fundamentals: while some of my mates pushed themselves to shred, I started trying to think more selflessly with the 6 strings. To be fair, some of my favourite guitarists do count as lead guitarists with incredible technical chops. As a teenager it was Ian D'sa of Billy Talent and Dave Baksh (Sum 41) -- Canadian powerhouses! As my music taste got heavier and went from pop punk to alternative to post-hardcore to metalcore, the ethos of "less might be more" started to really drive my approach to playing. I was enamoured with Brad Delson of Linkin Park -- I actually attribute my love of over-the-ear headphones to wanting to be just like him as a teen -- and his tasteful approach to playing. I couldn't understand how someone could alternate between the aggressiveness of Hybrid Theory and the wall of tone in Meteora while still having space for the melodies and chimes in Minutes to Midnight. To this day I'm not sure if it's a direct quote from him or a paraphrase on his Wikipedia page from back in the day, but learning that Delson disliked soloing and drawing attention to himself -- to use an awful pun -- struck a chord with me. And so that became my approach to playing: less is more.
